Exterior Paint & Shine

Before buying anything, it helps to know what you're actually choosing between. Wax, sealant, and ceramic coating get marketed as interchangeable — they aren't, and they last for very different lengths of time.

Wax vs. sealant vs. ceramic coating

Worth knowing

No spray-on product removes swirl marks, scratches, or oxidation. If the paint is already dull or scratched, that has to be corrected before any coating goes on — the coating locks in whatever's already there.

Consumer "ceramic spray" products sold in bottles are real chemistry, but diluted and far less durable than professional application. Months of protection is realistic; multi-year "permanent" claims usually aren't.

Paint correction, before any wax, sealant, or coating

  1. Wash and decontaminate first — you can't correct paint with embedded grit still on it without adding new scratches.
  2. Assess the damage in direct sunlight: light swirls, deeper scratches, and oxidation (chalky, faded paint) each need a different level of correction.
  3. Clay bar the paint to pull embedded contaminants — tar, rail dust, overspray — out of the clear coat, using a clay lubricant, never dry.
  4. Polish or compound with a dual-action polisher: a fine finishing polish for light swirls, a cutting compound followed by a finishing pass for deeper scratches or oxidation.
  5. Wipe down with isopropyl alcohol to remove polishing oils — leftover oil stops wax, sealant, or coating from bonding properly.
  6. Apply your chosen protection in thin, even coats, following that product's specific cure and buff time.

Realistic maintenance

  • Wash every 1–2 weeks so contaminants don't bond to whatever protection layer is on there.
  • Top up wax every 4–8 weeks, sealant every few months.
  • Ceramic coatings benefit from a "booster" spray every few washes to refresh hydrophobicity — the base coating itself doesn't need reapplying for years.

Full Exterior Detailing

The safe wash method (two-bucket method)

  1. Rinse the whole car first to knock off loose dirt before touching it with anything.
  2. Use two buckets, each fitted with a grit guard: one with soapy water, one with plain rinse water.
  3. Wash top-down in straight lines, not circles, with a microfiber wash mitt — dunk and rinse the mitt before reloading with soap so you're not grinding dirt back into the paint.
  4. Dry with a large microfiber towel or a leaf blower / air dryer to avoid water spots and micro-scratches from dragging cloth.

Wheels & tires

  • Wheels first, before the body — brake dust is acidic and abrasive.
  • Use a wheel-safe cleaner matched to your wheel finish; some acidic cleaners damage certain coatings or chrome.
  • Use a separate mitt and bucket for wheels — never the one used on paint.
  • Dress tires with a water-based, not solvent-based, dressing for a natural satin finish that won't sling onto paint or fade to brown or orange over time.

Trim, glass & headlights

  • Faded black plastic trim: clean thoroughly, then use a dedicated trim restorer or coating rather than a generic dressing, which fades fast.
  • Glass: a dedicated glass cleaner — ammonia-free if you have tint — and a separate microfiber towel, so paint-towel residue doesn't streak it.
  • Oxidized, yellowed headlights: wet-sand progressively from around 800 grit up to 3000+, polish, then finish with a UV-resistant clear coat — polishing alone without a UV topcoat lets them yellow again within months.
3

Full Interior Detailing

Work top to bottom and dry to wet — vacuum before anything wet touches a surface.

  1. Remove trash and personal items; pull the floor mats out.
  2. Vacuum everything first — seats, carpets, under seats, trunk — so you're not grinding grit into upholstery later.
  3. Dust and wipe hard surfaces: dashboard, console, door panels, vents (a soft detailing brush works well in vents and crevices).
  4. Clean glass from the inside with an ammonia-free cleaner if tinted.

Upholstery & carpets

  • Cloth seats and carpets: a dedicated fabric cleaner, agitated with a soft brush, extracted with a wet/dry vacuum rather than left to air-dry soaked — that's what causes a mildew smell.
  • Leather seats: a pH-balanced leather cleaner, not an all-purpose interior cleaner which can strip the finish, followed by a conditioner every 1–3 months depending on climate and sun exposure.
  • Stains: treat as soon as possible, blot rather than rub, and test any cleaner on a hidden area first.

Plastics & dashboard

Avoid high-gloss "tire shine"-style dressings on the dashboard — they attract dust and can cause windshield glare in sunlight. A satin-finish, water-based interior UV protectant works better.

Odor removal

Find and remove the source first — spilled food, mildew, smoke residue — since sprays and fresheners only mask odor. An ozone treatment or enzymatic odor eliminator actually breaks down the bacteria causing it; air freshener alone won't solve a persistent smell.

4

Mechanical Restoration

This is the part that determines whether the car is actually safe to drive — cosmetic work should never substitute for it on a car you intend to drive regularly.

Fluids & filters

  • Engine oil and filter, transmission fluid, coolant, brake fluid, power steering fluid — check condition and age, not just level. Dark or contaminated fluid gets flushed even if the level looks fine.
  • Replace the air filter and cabin air filter — cheap, and it directly affects performance and interior air quality.

Brakes & suspension

  • Inspect brake pads, rotors, and lines for wear, cracking, or leaks.
  • Check shocks, struts, bushings, and ball joints for play or leaks — worn suspension affects handling and tire wear even when the ride "feels fine."
  • Check tire condition, age, and alignment. Tires over roughly six years old should be inspected regardless of tread depth, since rubber degrades with age.

Electrical

  • Clean corrosion off battery terminals and check charge and load capacity.
  • Check every light — headlights, tail lights, turn signals, brake lights. Cheap fix, safety-critical.
  • Run an OBD-II scan for dashboard warning lights before doing any cosmetic work — an active check-engine or ABS light needs addressing first.

Undercarriage & rust

Inspect the undercarriage, wheel wells, and frame rails for surface rust versus structural rust. Surface rust can be treated; structural or perforating rust needs a body shop or fabrication assessment before the car is trusted on the road. Apply a rust inhibitor or undercoating after treatment, especially in climates with road salt.

Cosmetic Bodywork Restoration

Dents & dings

  • Small dents with no paint damage: paintless dent repair (PDR) is the least invasive option and preserves factory paint.
  • Dents with cracked or chipped paint: need traditional bodywork — filler, sanding, primer, paint — since PDR can't fix broken paint.

Rust repair

  1. Grind back to clean, solid metal — all of the rust, not just the visible flaking.
  2. Treat bare metal with a rust converter, or weld in patch panels if the rust has eaten through.
  3. Prime with an etching primer made for bare metal.
  4. Fill, sand, and blend into the surrounding panels before paint.

Paint & repaint

Small chips: touch-up paint matched to your VIN's paint code, found on a door-jamb sticker, applied in thin layers and leveled. Full panel or full-car repaint: color-matching, paint type, and booth conditions matter enormously — this is where DIY and professional results diverge the most, so get quotes from a body shop if the car has real value to you.

Trim & chrome restoration

Faded chrome: a dedicated chrome or metal polish — pitted chrome usually needs re-chroming rather than polishing. Faded plastic trim: light sanding, then a trim restorer or dye made for exterior plastic, not interior dressing.

Interior trim & upholstery restoration

Cracked dash or door panels can often be fixed with a vinyl/plastic crack repair kit, or full panel replacement for severe cases. Torn upholstery is best handled with professional reupholstering or seat covers matched to the original material for resale value.

6

Project Sequencing

On a full restoration, order matters — cosmetic work done before mechanical or structural work just gets redone later.

  1. Mechanical safety first: brakes, suspension, fluids, electrical, tires. Don't spend on paint for a car you can't safely drive yet.
  2. Structural and rust repair: fix anything affecting body integrity before bodywork or paint goes on top of it.
  3. Bodywork: dent repair, panel replacement, filler and sanding.
  4. Paint: primer, base coat, clear coat, or a professional respray.
  5. Correction and protection: polish, then wax, sealant, or ceramic coating, once paint has fully cured — professional paint typically needs 30+ days before polishing or coating.
  6. Interior restoration: upholstery, trim, electronics.
  7. Final detail pass: the full wash, wheels, glass, and interior detail from Sections 2 and 3 — done last so it isn't undone by messier earlier work.

How to Spot Inflated Marketing Claims

Worth keeping in mind while shopping for any of the products above — car-care marketing, spray coatings especially, leans on a few recurring patterns.

  • Invented-sounding proprietary technology names that aren't independent, verifiable chemistry terms — they're trademarked marketing language, not a claim you can check against a standard.
  • Anonymous or first-name-only testimonials with no way to verify they're real customers.
  • News-style article formatting for what's actually a paid ad — a real news article doesn't have a "Check Availability" button on every scroll.
  • Before/after photos shot in different lighting, angles, or wetness, which exaggerates the visible difference.
  • Comparisons only against the worst-case alternative — "hours of waxing" versus "two minutes" — rather than a fair equivalent product.
The takeaway

None of this means the product is necessarily useless — spray sealants and consumer ceramic sprays are real categories that do work to some degree. It just means the durability and performance numbers in an ad aren't tested fact. Check independent reviews — enthusiast forums, detailers who test multiple brands side by side — before buying.

Tools & Supplies Checklist

Washing

  • Two buckets + grit guards
  • pH-neutral car wash soap
  • Microfiber wash mitt (separate one for wheels)
  • Microfiber drying towels

Paint correction & protection

  • Clay bar + clay lubricant
  • Dual-action polisher
  • Cutting compound + finishing polish
  • Isopropyl alcohol panel wipe
  • Wax, sealant, or ceramic coating

Wheels, tires & trim

  • Wheel-safe cleaner
  • Soft-bristle, wheel-safe brush
  • Water-based tire dressing
  • Trim restorer

Interior

  • Vacuum with brush attachment
  • Soft detailing brushes
  • Upholstery/fabric cleaner
  • pH-balanced leather cleaner + conditioner
  • Ammonia-free glass cleaner
  • Water-based interior UV protectant

Mechanical

  • OBD-II scanner
  • Oil, coolant, brake fluid, power steering fluid
  • Torque wrench
  • Jack + jack stands

Bodywork

  • Rust converter / etching primer
  • Body filler + progressive-grit sanding blocks
  • Touch-up paint matched to VIN paint code

This guide is general reference information, not a substitute for a qualified mechanic or body shop assessment — especially for anything involving brakes, suspension, structural rust, or frame integrity.
